About

Building Trust in Climate and Weather Models

A model that predicts well and explains nothing will be checked, doubted, and eventually set aside. That gap between statistical skill and operational use is what my doctoral research works on, and the Indian monsoon is where it is tested.

The setting makes the case. An agriculture-dependent economy supports 1.4 billion people, and the monsoon sets the terms each year. The 2015 drought lasted 41 months and affected 330 million people, while floods claimed 45,065 lives between 1995 and 2020. In collaboration with Dr. Vivek Gupta at IIT Mandi, I am building a single integrated forecasting framework across the full hazard spectrum, ensuring drought and flood are handled by one cohesive system rather than two isolated ones.

Trust is what the framework is organised around. High-capacity models are opaque wherever they are deployed, and a warning nobody can explain is a warning nobody acts on. Every model is therefore built to forecast and to show its reasoning, using attention weights, variable-selection networks and calibrated uncertainty. Each is then tested on data it has never seen, including precipitation products with different resolutions and observational histories, so transferability is demonstrated rather than assumed.

The work spans the whole chain, from hydro-meteorological drivers and deep-learning architectures through hydrodynamic modelling to reservoir operations, where a forecast finally becomes a decision. That chain is the same wherever models feed water and weather management, which is what makes methods proven against the monsoon useful well beyond it.

Doctoral research

Advancing multi-scale hydro-meteorological hazard monitoring and prediction over India

Five studies that move from trust and transferability, through scale, to action — integrating spatiotemporal attention, interpretability and transfer learning. Click any figure to enlarge.

01

Trust · Why the model believes what it believes

Interpretable Deep Learning for National-Scale Drought Forecasting

Earlier Indian drought deep-learning studies were station-specific or confined to a single region, and the high-capacity models they used were opaque. This study asks whether one model can forecast drought across the whole of India and show its reasoning. A Temporal Fusion Transformer is trained on IMDAA meteorology (0.12°) plus NOAA climate indices to predict SPEI-3 at 30-, 60- and 90-day leads, with variable-selection networks and attention weights exposing the drivers, and quantile outputs supplying uncertainty.

  • First use of interpretable transformers for drought forecasting, validated across six Köppen–Geiger climatic zones covering all of India.
  • Attention concentrates on immediate antecedent conditions and the preceding 12-month monsoon cycle — the model rediscovers hydrological memory unprompted.
  • PET outweighs raw temperature as a driver: atmospheric water demand is a stronger drought signal than heat alone.
  • The Indian Ocean Dipole is the most influential large-scale driver of Indian drought — more so than any ENSO variable.
  • Latitude and longitude are the strongest static predictors, with sensitivity shifting by whether a region is tropical, hilly or arid.

02

Transferability · One backbone, eleven products

Cross-Product Transferability of the Drought Forecasting Framework

An operational forecasting system cannot be retrained every time the input data changes. India's precipitation record is fragmented across satellite, reanalysis, gauge-based and blended products at different resolutions and instruments. This study takes the TFT backbone pre-trained on IMDAA (1979–2014) and applies it zero-shot — no fine-tuning, no target-domain adaptation — to eleven heterogeneous precipitation products.

  • Most datasets held ΔRMSE and ΔMAE within ±0.05 and ΔNSE within ±0.1, demonstrating stable transferability without retraining.
  • ERA5 and MSWEP transferred most strongly, matching source-domain forecasting performance.
  • Zero-shot skill peaks in winter and pre-monsoon (correlations > 0.7) and declines through the high-variability monsoon.
  • Establishes a practical route to operational drought forecasting where a single pre-trained model serves whichever product is available.
JGR: Machine Learning & Computation · 2026 Zero-shot transfer learning 11 precipitation products ERA5 · MSWEP · CHIRPS · IMERG
03

Scale · When drought arrives in days

Pentad-Scale Flash Drought Characterization and Prediction for India

Flash droughts intensify in weeks rather than months and are devastating for agriculture, yet Indian studies have leaned on model-derived soil moisture that carries heavy parametric uncertainty and is validated by sparse in-situ measurements. This work introduces a purely meteorological characterization — SPEI computed at a pentad (5-day) scale, SPEI-5d — and benchmarks six custom deep-learning architectures against a time-series foundation model.

  • First comprehensive meteorological flash-drought framework for India, independent of soil-moisture products.
  • PET-formulation sensitivity tested against SMAP soil moisture: Hargreaves achieved higher event-capture ratios than Penman–Monteith across India, 2016–2020.
  • Benchmarked LSTM, BiLSTM, BiGRU, KAN, N-HiTS and PatchTST against the TimeGPT foundation model over a 1979–2020 climatology.
  • A two-stage operational design: TFT for high-precision short leads (1–3 days), PatchTST for sustained 5–10-day outlooks.

04

Scale · From hazard indices to the weather that makes them

SwinFusion — A Regional Weather Forecasting Framework over India

Global AI weather models are locked behind supercomputers and run at six-hourly cadence. SwinFusion is a limited-area alternative: a Swin-Transformer U-Net over the Indian subcontinent at 0.25° (~28 km), carrying 21 atmospheric variables, running hourly and autoregressively to +48 h, with physics guards keeping precipitation non-negative and humidity bounded. A residual corrector and a residual diffusion stage then convert the single deterministic map into a 32-member probabilistic ensemble. It trains on a single NVIDIA A5000 12 GB GPU — a cost a regional lab can afford.

  • Deterministic verification on the fully held-out year 2020 (363 daily launches, WeatherBench-2 convention): MSLP 0.45 → 0.88 hPa and T2m 0.83 → 1.35 K from 6 h to 48 h.
  • Beats Graph-LAM for every variable at every lead (z500 −20 %, MSLP −15 % at 24 h) under identical data, boundaries and loss.
  • Beats Pangu-Weather at all leads for MSLP and low-level geopotential — the workstation model wins near the surface, where hazards live. GraphCast remains stronger aloft and for precipitation; no claim is made to beat the global state of the art.
  • Diffusion improves CRPS by 31.6 % over raw Swin-LAM and more than doubles the Brier skill score at the 1 mm h⁻¹ threshold (0.19 → 0.42), with ROC 0.95 / 0.93 at 1 / 5 mm h⁻¹.
  • Cyclone tracking against IBTrACS for Amphan and Nisarga: pooled position error 49 km at +6 h rising to 136 km at +48 h; day-ahead landfall error 123 km (Amphan, 36 h) and 60 km (Nisarga, 33 h).
  • At basin scale with no basin-specific training, the ensemble band brackets observed peaks a day ahead where the deterministic run flattens them by a third.

05

Action · Where prediction meets decision

An Integrated Forecast-to-Action Framework for the August 2023 Punjab Floods

A forecast only matters if it changes a decision. In August 2023, Punjab received 111.9 mm of rain in four days against a normal of 41.7 mm — a 168 % deviation — while 34 landslides and 6 cloudbursts struck Himachal Pradesh. Pong Dam inflow climbed from 4,058 cumec on 13 August to a peak of 12,469 cumec on 15 August; 12,000 villages were affected and 65 people died. This study unifies observational, reanalysis and demographic data into one framework that couples regional weather forecasting, HEC-RAS 2D hydrodynamic modelling, demographic vulnerability assessment and multi-objective reservoir optimization.

  • A penalty-based genetic algorithm updates the daily release strategy, with a piecewise penalty function that heavily penalises breaching minimum storage or pushing water toward the maximum stage limit.
  • Optimized operations are compared against actual dam control across several maximum-stage scenarios (419 m, 420 m, 422 m, 424 m), quantifying the attenuation that better-informed releases could have achieved.
  • Swin-LAM-driven outflow control is benchmarked against GFS-driven control, closing the loop from the regional weather model of Chapter 4 to reservoir action.
  • Human exposure is quantified by intersecting simulated inundation with village-level Census 2011 demographics — resilience assessed in the Anthropocene, not just hydrology.
  • Supported by the UK FCDO under the CARA programme, in collaboration with the British Geological Survey and the National Institute of Hydrology.
